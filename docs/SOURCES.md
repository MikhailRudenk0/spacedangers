### 1. NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center — services.swpc.noaa.gov — 75.0 / 75 (A — build on it) · *provisional, not yet probed*

**NOAA / US National Weather Service** · https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/  
**Covers:** Solar activity, Geomagnetic, Radiation environment, Ionosphere  
**Auth:** none · **Formats:** json, text · **Updates:** minutes · **Archive:** decades · **Licence:** Public domain (US Government)  
**Verification:** not measured (10 probes blocked by local network) · latency n/a

The operational space-weather feed. No key, no rate limit, updates every
1–5 minutes, and it carries both the raw measurements and the official
warnings:
* alerts.json — the actual issued Watches, Warnings and Alerts (geomagnetic
  storm G1–G5, radio blackout R1–R5, radiation storm S1–S5), with issue
  time and free-text product.
* noaa-scales.json — current and 3-day forecast R/S/G scale values.
* GOES X-ray flux (1-minute, 0.05–0.4 nm and 0.1–0.8 nm) — this is how you
  detect a solar flare and classify it A/B/C/M/X.
* GOES integral proton flux at >10/>50/>100 MeV — solar radiation storms,
  the thing that threatens astronauts, polar flights and satellite parts.
* GOES integral/differential electrons — surface and deep-dielectric
  charging risk for spacecraft.
* DSCOVR/ACE real-time solar wind at L1 — density, speed, temperature and
  the interplanetary magnetic field including Bz, which is the single best
  ~30–60 minute lead indicator of a geomagnetic storm.
* Planetary K index (1-minute estimated and 3-hourly), Kp forecast.
* OVATION aurora nowcast grid, D-region absorption, 3-day forecast text.
* Solar-cycle indices: sunspot number and F10.7 monthly back to 1749.

### 3. NASA/JPL Solar System Dynamics & CNEOS API — 71.0 / 75 (A — build on it) · *provisional, not yet probed*

**NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory** · https://ssd-api.jpl.nasa.gov/doc/  
**Covers:** NEO / impact risk, Fireballs & bolides, Orbits & ephemerides  
**Auth:** none · **Formats:** json · **Updates:** daily · **Archive:** decades · **Licence:** Public domain (US Government)  
**Verification:** not measured (5 probes blocked by local network) · latency n/a

The authoritative machine-readable feed for asteroid and comet hazard.
Four distinct products behind one host:
* Close-Approach Data (CAD): every known close approach of an asteroid or
  comet to Earth (or any planet) between 1900 and 2200 — date, nominal and
  minimum distance in au / lunar distances, relative velocity, absolute
  magnitude H, and the 3-sigma uncertainty on time of approach.
* Sentry: the impact-risk table. Per object: number of potential impacts,
  impact probability, year range, Palermo and Torino scale ratings,
  estimated diameter and impact energy in megatons.
* Fireball: energy and location of atmospheric bolide detections by US
  government sensors since 1988 — date, lat/lon, altitude, velocity vector,
  total radiated energy and computed impact energy.
* SBDB / SBDB Query: full orbital elements, covariance, physical parameters
  (diameter, albedo, rotation, spectral type) and discovery circumstances
  for ~1.4M small bodies; the query endpoint does server-side filtering so
  you can pull "all PHAs with H < 22" in one request.

The keyless front door to the US catalogue of tracked objects, and the one
most projects actually build on:
* GP element sets for ~28,000 tracked objects in TLE, 3LE, JSON, CSV or OMM
  format, grouped (active, starlink, last-30-days, cosmos-2251-debris,
  iridium-33-debris, ...) or queried by NORAD ID / name. Feed these to an
  SGP4 propagator and you have every satellite and debris position.
* SATCAT — the object catalogue: international designator, launch date and
  site, object type, RCS size class, current orbital parameters, decay date
  for objects that have reentered, and operational status.
* SOCRATES — twice-daily all-on-all conjunction screening of the public
  catalogue: object pairs, time of closest approach, minimum range,
  relative velocity, maximum probability of collision.
* Space-weather CSV — historical and predicted Ap, Kp, F10.7 in exactly the
  form atmospheric-drag models want, which is what turns a TLE into a
  reentry-date estimate.
